\begin{abstract}
TabPFN classifies by in-context learning: it conditions on a set of labeled
training rows (the context, or prototypes) and predicts test labels without
gradient updates. On small tabular datasets, where TabPFN is designed to be
used, the practitioner still chooses how large that context is and which rows
go into it. We ask how much those choices matter. Using repeated random
sub-sampling on six small OpenML datasets, we study three linked questions:
(i) how variable predictions are across different random contexts of the same
size, and whether larger contexts are more stable; (ii) whether accuracy tracks
how well a sampled context preserves the full training distribution; and
(iii) whether more expensive selection (K-Means, farthest-point sampling) buys
anything over uniform random selection once the context is representative.
We find that larger contexts are both more accurate and markedly more stable
(the coefficient of variation of AUC falls from around 8--13\% at $k{=}16$ to
under 2\% at $k{=}256$ on the datasets with room to improve); that accuracy
tracks how well a context preserves the full training distribution (on all six
datasets the most-representative third of random draws outperforms the
least-representative third, and feature-mean shift correlates negatively with
AUC, up to Pearson $r=-0.79$); and that K-Means and farthest-point selection
give accuracy within about one AUC point of uniform random while costing two to
three orders of magnitude more to compute.
This is a small-scale study (six datasets, CPU), so we describe conclusions as
observations rather than established laws.

\textbf{Keywords:} in-context learning, TabPFN, prototype sampling, context
stability, small data
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An in-context learner like TabPFN takes a set of labeled examples as input and
produces a predictor in a single forward pass. The set it conditions on---the
context---is chosen at inference time: how many rows ($k$) and which rows. On
large datasets the interesting question is how to fit within a compute budget.
On \emph{small} datasets, the regime TabPFN targets, the question is different
and more basic: given that we could pass all the data, how much does the
\emph{choice} of context actually change the model's behavior?

We investigate three connected hypotheses on six small OpenML datasets
(all with fewer than about 1{,}000 training rows):

\begin{description}
\item[H1 (Stability).] Different random contexts of the same size $k$ yield
different predictions; the spread of accuracy across random draws shrinks as $k$
grows.
\item[H2 (Representativeness).] A context that better preserves the full
training distribution yields higher accuracy; this is why uniform random
sampling works well.
\item[H3 (Selection cost).] Once a context is representative, expensive
selection methods add little over random selection.
\end{description}

These are mechanistic questions about \emph{how} TabPFN responds to its context,
not benchmark claims, so they can be studied with a few well-chosen datasets and
many repeated draws rather than a large dataset suite. We also report two
supporting stress tests: duplicating part of the context (redundancy) and
dropping part of it (robustness).

We state limitations plainly. Six datasets is a small sample; runs are CPU-only;
and we do not prove mechanisms, only measure behavior consistent with them.

\section{Setup}

We use TabPFN v3 with \texttt{ignore\_pretraining\_limits=True}. Each dataset is
split 80/20 (stratified, fixed split seed). A context of size $k$ is formed by
selecting $k$ training rows and fitting TabPFN on exactly those rows
(\texttt{clf.fit(X[idx], y[idx])}); the held-out 20\% is the test set throughout.
We report ROC AUC (one-vs-rest macro for the multiclass dataset). For the
stability and representativeness analyses we draw \textbf{20 independent random
contexts} per $k$; for selection-method and stress tests we use three seeds.

\textbf{Representativeness metrics.} For a sampled context we measure two simple
distances from the full training set: (i) the total-variation distance between
the context's class distribution and the full class distribution
(\texttt{class\_tv}); and (ii) the mean absolute difference between the
context's standardized per-feature means and the full training means
(\texttt{feat\_shift}). Smaller values mean the context looks more like the
whole training set.

\textbf{What we observe.} On every dataset, variability across random contexts
falls as $k$ grows. On the four datasets with room to improve (diabetes,
credit-g, vehicle, blood-transfusion) the CV drops from roughly 8--13\% at
$k{=}16$ to about 1--3\% at $k{=}256$; on the two already near ceiling
(breast-w, wdbc) it is small throughout and still shrinks. Mean AUC rises with
$k$ in parallel. So on small data the context size is not only an
accuracy knob but a \emph{reliability} knob: a small context makes the model's
answer depend heavily on which rows happened to be drawn, and enlarging the
context removes most of that dependence. The single most variable configuration
we saw was diabetes at $k{=}16$, where AUC over 20 draws ranged from 0.497 to
0.842.

\section{H2: Representativeness}

Using the same 20 random draws per $k$, we ask whether the draws that better
preserve the full training distribution are the ones that score higher. For each
draw we computed two distances to the full training set: the total-variation
distance between class distributions (\texttt{class\_tv}) and the mean absolute
standardized feature-mean shift (\texttt{feat\_shift}). We then correlated each
distance with AUC. Table~\ref{tab:repr} reports Pearson $r$, Spearman $\rho$,
and the linear $R^2$ per dataset, pooling over all $k$.

\textbf{What we observe.} The direction predicted by H2 holds everywhere: every
correlation in Table~\ref{tab:repr} is negative (further from the full
distribution $\rightarrow$ lower AUC), and in Table~\ref{tab:contrast} the
most-representative third of draws beats the least-representative third on all
six datasets. The \emph{strength}, however, is dataset-dependent.
\texttt{feat\_shift} is the better predictor of the two, and it is strong where
the dataset has accuracy headroom---credit-g ($r=-0.79$, $R^2=0.62$), vehicle
($r=-0.77$, $R^2=0.59$), diabetes ($r=-0.55$)---and weak on datasets that sit
near the ceiling (breast-w, wdbc) or near chance (blood-transfusion), where AUC
barely varies and there is little for representativeness to explain. Pooling
across datasets (z-scoring AUC within each so levels are comparable),
\texttt{feat\_shift} reaches Pearson $-0.50$ / Spearman $-0.59$.

This is the paper's central observation, stated at the strength the data
supports: on small datasets, \emph{how representative} the context is explains a
substantial part of the accuracy spread among equally sized random contexts,
especially the feature-distribution match. It offers a mechanism for why uniform
random sampling is a good default---random draws are, in expectation,
distribution-preserving---rather than a coincidence. We did not prove causation;
we measured that representativeness and accuracy move together in the direction
the hypothesis predicts.

\textbf{What we observe.} Accuracy differences between the three methods are
small---within about one to three AUC points, and not in a consistent direction
(K-Means was marginally ahead on diabetes, credit-g, and blood-transfusion;
FPS or random on the others). On the two datasets where K-Means led by a couple
of points (credit-g, blood-transfusion), the random runs also had the largest
seed-to-seed spread, so at least part of the gap is noise. Meanwhile the compute
cost is very different: random selection took on the order of $0.0003$\,s,
versus $\sim$0.27\,s for K-Means---roughly three orders of magnitude---and up to
about a second on one dataset. Given near-equal accuracy, the extra machinery of
K-Means or FPS bought little here over drawing rows at random.

\section{Supporting: Redundancy and Robustness}

Starting from a random context of $k{=}128$, we either duplicated a fraction of
its rows (adding no new information) or dropped a fraction of them.

\textbf{Redundancy.} Duplicating rows had little effect on most datasets: on
diabetes, vehicle, wdbc, and breast-w, AUC stayed within about half a point up
to 50--70\% duplication. The exception was blood-transfusion, where duplication
steadily hurt (0.686 at 0\% down to 0.580 at 70\%), and to a lesser extent
credit-g. So duplicate context rows are mostly harmless when the underlying
signal is strong, but on a weak-signal dataset they skew the context and degrade
predictions.

\textbf{Robustness.} Dropping rows degraded accuracy gradually rather than
sharply. Removing 30\% of a 128-row context cost roughly 0--1.5 AUC points on the
stronger datasets (wdbc, breast-w, vehicle, diabetes) and more on the weaker
ones; only at 50\% removal did larger drops appear (e.g.\ blood-transfusion
$0.686\rightarrow0.614$). This is consistent with H1: with fewer rows the context
carries less of the distribution and predictions get both worse and noisier.

\section{Discussion}

The three results fit together into one account of context sampling on small
data. H1 shows that a small context makes TabPFN's prediction unstable---the
answer depends on which rows were drawn---and that enlarging the context removes
most of that instability while also raising accuracy. H2 gives a reason: among
equally sized random contexts, the ones that better match the full training
distribution (particularly in their feature means) tend to score higher, and the
most-representative draws beat the least-representative on every dataset. A
larger context helps in part because it is, on average, more representative and
less subject to an unlucky draw. H3 closes the loop for practice: since uniform
random sampling is distribution-preserving in expectation, it already captures
most of what representativeness offers, so the more expensive selection methods
we tried added little accuracy at much higher cost.

Taken together, on small tabular datasets the useful thing to control is the
\emph{representativeness and size} of the context, not the sophistication of the
selection algorithm. Uniform random sampling at a sufficiently large $k$ is a
strong and cheap default. We state this as an observation from six datasets, not
a general law.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item Six small datasets; results may not extend to larger or higher-dimensional
data.
\item CPU-only; timing comparisons are CPU costs.
\item Representativeness is measured with two coarse distances; richer distances
(e.g.\ full distributional divergences) are future work.
\item We observe behavior consistent with the hypotheses but do not prove the
underlying mechanism.
\end{itemize}
