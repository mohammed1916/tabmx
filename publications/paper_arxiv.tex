\begin{abstract}
TabPFN performs tabular classification by in-context learning over a set of
training (prototype) samples. Two practical questions follow from this design:
how many prototypes are needed, and how they should be chosen. This report
collects a set of small-scale experiments that measure, on a handful of public
OpenML datasets, (i) how full-context TabPFN accuracy changes as the training
set grows, (ii) how accuracy changes with the prototype count $k$ on one
held-out dataset, and (iii) how several prototype-selection strategies compare
in accuracy and selection time. We report only what we measured. The
measurements are limited in scale (most claims rest on two to six datasets, and
runs were performed on CPU except where noted), so we describe the results as
observations and intuitions rather than established findings. All numbers come
directly from the logged experiment outputs.

\textbf{Keywords:} in-context learning, prototype selection, context size,
tabular learning, empirical study
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

TabPFN classifies test rows by attending over a set of labeled training rows
supplied as context. The number of context rows ($k$) and the way they are
selected are the two knobs a practitioner controls at inference time. This
report documents a series of small experiments probing those two knobs. It is
an empirical log, not a theory paper: every number below is read directly from
an experiment output file, and we avoid claims that go beyond the data we
collected.

We emphasize the limitations up front. The experiments cover at most six
datasets; several rest on only one or two. Most runs were executed on CPU, so
absolute timings are slow and are not comparable to GPU deployment. We did not
repeat every configuration across many seeds. Accordingly, we present the
results as observations and possible intuitions, and we flag where the evidence
is thin.

\section{Setup}

We use TabPFN v3 with \texttt{ignore\_pretraining\_limits=True}. Datasets are
public OpenML tasks. Unless stated otherwise, splits are stratified and the
random seed is fixed. We report ROC AUC (one-vs-rest macro for multiclass).
The device used for each experiment is stated in the corresponding table,
because it differed between experiments and affects timing.

\section{Observation 1: Full-Context Accuracy vs. Training-Set Size}

We measured full-context TabPFN, a fixed-$k{=}512$ prototype variant, and a
tuned LightGBM baseline as the training set grows, on two datasets
(bank-marketing, adult), single seed, on CPU.

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{What we observe.}
\begin{itemize}
\item On both datasets, full-context AUC rises with $n$ and the gains shrink as
$n$ grows. On bank-marketing, AUC moves from 0.9159 at $n{=}1{,}000$ to 0.9439
at $n{=}10{,}000$, then to 0.9465 at $n{=}40{,}689$: most of the improvement
occurs by $n{\approx}10{,}000$. We describe this as diminishing returns, not as
a proven saturation law.
\item Full-context wall-clock time grew steeply on CPU (from tens of seconds to
$\sim$2{,}500--3{,}000 s at the largest sizes). LightGBM ran in under 5 seconds
throughout and reached competitive AUC (on adult it exceeded full TabPFN at
$n\geq5{,}000$ in these runs).
\item The fixed-$k{=}512$ prototype variant did not track full-context accuracy
as $n$ grew; it stayed near 0.89--0.90. This is consistent with the idea that a
fixed small context is not enough for the larger datasets here.
\end{itemize}

These runs were CPU-only, so the timings reflect CPU cost and should not be
read as GPU deployment numbers.

\section{Observation 2: Accuracy vs.\ Prototype Count $k$}

On the electricity dataset (held out from the meta-model in Observation~3), we
swept $k$ with random prototype selection.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{ROC AUC vs.\ prototype count $k$ on electricity ($n_{\text{train}}
=40{,}780$), random selection.}
\label{tab:ksweep}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
$k$ & ROC AUC \\
\midrule
256  & 0.8483 \\
512  & 0.8606 \\
1024 & 0.8887 \\
2048 & 0.9181 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{paper_figures/fig2_k_sweep.pdf}
\caption{ROC AUC vs.\ prototype count $k$ on electricity. Accuracy keeps rising
through $k{=}2048$; no plateau is seen in the tested range.}
\label{fig:ksweep}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What we observe.} On this one dataset, AUC increased with $k$ across the
range tested; we did not observe a plateau within $k\leq2048$. So for a large
dataset like electricity, a small fixed $k$ (e.g.\ 512) left accuracy on the
table relative to $k{=}2048$ (0.8606 vs.\ 0.9181, a difference of about
$+6.7\%$). This is a single-dataset observation.

\subsection{A dataset-dependent $k$}

We fit a small linear meta-model mapping dataset meta-features
($\log n$, $\log m$, number of classes, class density, $n/m$ ratio, sparsity,
test size) to a per-dataset ``optimal'' $k$ chosen by validation AUC on five
training datasets, then applied it to electricity as a held-out test.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Per-dataset validation-optimal $k$ (five training datasets) and the
held-out test.}
\label{tab:adaptive}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Dataset & role & optimal $k$ (val.) \\
\midrule
diabetes       & train & 64 \\
vehicle        & train & 1024 \\
credit-g       & train & 512 \\
bank-marketing & train & 2048 \\
adult          & train & 2048 \\
\midrule
electricity    & test  & 2048 (predicted) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

On electricity, using the predicted $k{=}2048$ rather than a fixed $k{=}512$
raised AUC from 0.8606 to 0.9181 ($+6.7\%$) in this single held-out test. The
pattern in the training data---small datasets favor small $k$, large datasets
favor larger $k$---is intuitive but rests on only five training points and one
test point, so we treat ``$k$ can be predicted from dataset properties'' as a
hypothesis supported by a single successful held-out case, not as a validated
result.

\section{Observation 3: Prototype Selection Method}

We compared prototype-selection strategies at a fixed $k$. Two datasets
(diabetes, vehicle) were tested across random, K-Means, farthest-point sampling
(FPS), and class-balanced K-Means at $k{=}512$ (three seeds, CPU). We also ran
random vs.\ K-Means at $k{=}2048$ on electricity.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Selection method comparison. AUC values are means over three seeds
(diabetes, vehicle) or single runs (electricity). Selection time is the time to
choose the prototypes, separate from inference.}
\label{tab:selection}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Dataset & Method & ROC AUC & selection time (s) \\
\midrule
diabetes ($k{=}512$) & random          & 0.9081 & $\sim$0.0002 \\
diabetes ($k{=}512$) & FPS             & 0.9143 & $\sim$0.01 \\
diabetes ($k{=}512$) & K-Means         & 0.9165 & $\sim$0.15--3.1 \\
diabetes ($k{=}512$) & K-Means (bal.)  & 0.9116 & $\sim$0.18 \\
\midrule
vehicle ($k{=}512$)  & random          & 0.9740 & $\sim$0.0002 \\
vehicle ($k{=}512$)  & FPS             & 0.9781 & $\sim$0.02 \\
vehicle ($k{=}512$)  & K-Means         & 0.9762 & $\sim$0.25 \\
vehicle ($k{=}512$)  & K-Means (bal.)  & 0.9778 & $\sim$0.25 \\
\midrule
electricity ($k{=}2048$) & random      & 0.9181 & $\sim$0.0002 \\
electricity ($k{=}2048$) & K-Means      & 0.9078 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{What we observe.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Across these datasets, the accuracy differences between random, K-Means,
FPS, and balanced K-Means were small (within roughly one AUC point). On diabetes
and vehicle, K-Means and FPS were marginally ahead; on electricity at
$k{=}2048$, random was marginally ahead. We do not read these small differences
as a consistent ranking.
\item Random selection was much cheaper to compute: its selection time was on
the order of $10^{-4}$ s, versus roughly $0.15$--$3$ s for K-Means---about three
orders of magnitude faster in these runs. Because it costs almost nothing and
matched the other methods on accuracy here, random is a reasonable default.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Selection strategies that hurt}

Two ``informed'' strategies performed clearly worse than random at $k{=}512$:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Informed selection vs.\ random at $k{=}512$. Negative change means the
method underperformed random.}
\label{tab:badselect}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Dataset & Method & AUC (method) & AUC (random) \\
\midrule
electricity    & entropy (uncertainty)    & 0.6348 & 0.8606 \\
bank-marketing & entropy (uncertainty)    & 0.6726 & 0.9023 \\
electricity    & feature-variance (imp.)  & 0.7295 & 0.8606 \\
bank-marketing & feature-variance (imp.)  & 0.8749 & 0.9023 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Selecting high-uncertainty (entropy) prototypes dropped AUC sharply
($-26\%$ and $-25\%$ on the two datasets), and feature-variance selection also
hurt ($-15\%$ and $-3\%$). A plausible intuition is that these strategies pick
non-representative subsets, and TabPFN's in-context learning appears to prefer a
context that resembles the overall training distribution. We did not directly
measure distribution similarity, so this remains an intuition.

\centering

\caption{Selection methods at $k{=}512$. Accuracy differences are small (left);

\end{figure}

\section{Observation 4: Splitting the Context Across Heads}

We tested splitting a fixed budget of $k_{\text{total}}{=}1024$ prototypes
across $1, 2, 4, 8$ independent ``heads'' and averaging predictions, on
electricity and bank-marketing.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Mean ROC AUC as the fixed budget $k_{\text{total}}{=}1024$ is split
across heads.}
\label{tab:multihead}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Dataset & 1 head & 2 heads & 4 heads & 8 heads \\
\midrule
electricity    & 0.8887 & 0.8661 & 0.8530 & 0.8320 \\
bank-marketing & 0.9193 & 0.8982 & 0.8664 & 0.8439 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{paper_figures/fig4_multihead.pdf}
\caption{Mean AUC as a fixed budget $k_{\text{total}}{=}1024$ is split across
more heads. Accuracy falls monotonically on both datasets.}
\label{fig:multihead}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What we observe.} On both datasets, splitting the budget monotonically
reduced accuracy: one head with all 1024 prototypes did best. This suggests that
a single larger context is preferable to several smaller ones at fixed total
budget, at least in these two cases.

\section{Observation 5: Behavior Under Input Shift}

We applied synthetic feature-scale and additive-noise shifts to the test inputs
and measured the AUC drop relative to the unshifted baseline, for full-context
and fixed-$k{=}512$ prototype TabPFN, on four datasets.

\textbf{What we observe.} Scale shifts degraded accuracy much more than noise
shifts. For example, a $5\times$ feature-scale shift reduced AUC substantially
on diabetes (about $-53\%$ full, $-61\%$ proto) and vehicle
($\approx-47\%$ both), while additive noise caused smaller drops. On
bank-marketing the full model degraded more than the proto model under the
$5\times$ scale shift ($-26\%$ vs.\ $-7\%$); we do not have enough datasets to
say whether this ordering is general. These are stress tests with artificial
shifts and should be read as such.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scale.} At most six datasets are used; several observations rest
on one or two. Differences of about one AUC point are within the noise we would
expect at this scale.
\item \textbf{Device.} Most runs were CPU-only (the scaling and selection
timing experiments). Timings are therefore CPU costs and are not representative
of GPU inference.
\item \textbf{Seeds.} Seed coverage varies by experiment (one to five seeds);
not every configuration was repeated.
\item \textbf{No mechanism.} Where we note intuitions (why $k$ scales with $n$,
why representative selection helps), we did not test the mechanism directly.
\item \textbf{Meta-model.} The $k$-prediction meta-model was fit on five
datasets and validated on one; this is a feasibility check, not a validated
predictor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

From these small experiments we take the following intuitions, stated at the
strength the data supports:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Full-context TabPFN accuracy improves with training size but with
diminishing returns; most of the gain on our two scaling datasets arrived by
$\sim$10{,}000 rows.
\item The useful prototype count $k$ appears dataset-dependent: on a large
dataset (electricity), raising $k$ from 512 to 2048 helped noticeably; on small
datasets a small $k$ sufficed.
\item Among the selection methods tested, accuracy differences were small,
while random selection was far cheaper---so random is a sensible default.
Uncertainty- and variance-based selection performed markedly worse.
\item At a fixed budget, one large context beat several smaller ones.
\end{enumerate}

These are observations from a limited set of runs. Confirming them would require
more datasets, GPU timing, and repeated seeds.
